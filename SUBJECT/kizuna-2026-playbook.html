KIZUNA 2026: the 25-hour hackathon winning playbook

Approach this as a competition, not a university assignment. 25 hours, a team of four, ESPRIT, themes Water, Energy and Agriculture. 10 finalist places; the goal is to make the shortlist and win.

The team that writes the most code does not necessarily win. The team that identifies the right problem, builds the clearest working solution, proves its value and communicates it convincingly has a much better chance. Aim for the most convincing complete product, not the biggest project.

1. What the research says about winning

Sources: organizer guidance, judging rubrics, university guides, pitch resources and participant accounts, including How I Won 11 Hackathons, the MLH judging rules, HackUTD's pitching guide and Harvard's Hackathon Participant Guide. MLH criteria are not KIZUNA's confirmed criteria, but several principles recur.

Seven principles

  1. Solve a specific problem, not an entire industry. One identifiable problem for one identifiable user, not "revolutionizing Tunisian agriculture".
  2. A complete MVP beats an ambitious unfinished platform. One working end-to-end journey beats 15 disconnected features.
  3. Integrate early. Don't build four separate projects until the last three hours.
  4. Demonstrate measurable value. Show how much water you could save under a clearly stated scenario, with a transparent calculation.
  5. Make the demo the star. Judges should see the product working, not hear an architecture lecture.
  6. Use mentors strategically. Validate your problem, assumptions and approach early.
  7. Protect the final hours. Stop adding features, then test, fix, rehearse and prepare a backup demo.

MLH's framework weighs technology, design, completion and learning equally and encourages showing what was actually built. Don't sacrifice functionality for presentation theatre.

2. Understand what KIZUNA is looking for

The official description emphasizes concrete solutions with measurable impact, supported by technical mentors and JICA experts.

TrackProblems worth exploring
WaterWater losses, irrigation optimization, reuse, intelligent management
EnergyConsumption optimization, renewable energy, energy efficiency
AgricultureSmart farming, resource allocation, IoT and AI

Recommendation: don't automatically choose the AI-heavy idea. Choose the one linking a real problem, an achievable technical solution and measurable impact. Wait for the actual challenge statement. Possible directions:

  • A simple predictive irrigation tool with a working recommendation engine may beat an elaborate AI agricultural platform.
  • An energy dashboard that finds waste and suggests specific interventions may beat a chatbot.
  • A water-loss detector with a transparent anomaly pipeline may beat a generic water app.

First mission: understand the problem better than other teams

Don't start coding. Spend the first 30-45 minutes establishing:

  1. Who experiences the problem?
  2. What exactly happens?
  3. Why does it happen?
  4. How is it handled now?
  5. Why is the current solution insufficient?
  6. What data is available?
  7. What can we realistically demo in 25 hours?
  8. What measurable outcome would prove it works?

Talk to the mentors, especially domain experts. Ask which real-world constraints teams usually underestimate.

Recommended architecture

Frontend (React: input, dashboard, charts, results) → Backend (FastAPI: API, validation, business logic) → AI/ML (Python: model, inference) → Database (storage, history). Add sensor/IoT integration only if the challenge needs it. Each person works independently while interfaces stay stable.

Establish the API contract immediately

{ "input": { "soil_moisture": 28, "temperature": 32, "humidity": 45 } }

{ "prediction": "irrigation_required", "confidence": 0.91,
  "recommended_action": "Irrigate now" }

Illustrative values and a sample contract, not real model outputs.

This prevents the classic disaster: frontend expects one JSON shape, backend returns another, AI uses a third.

Git rules

  • One shared repository; one main branch that always runs.
  • Feature branches: frontend/dashboard, backend/api, ai/model, infra/deployment.
  • Small, frequent commits; reviews before merging; never overwrite others' work.
  • Keep a working previous version before major changes.
Rule: nobody merges a change into the working application without testing the affected user flow.

5. Building a project that impresses judges

A. Problem understanding

Weak: "We want to use AI to optimize water consumption."

Strong: "Small agricultural operators often decide on irrigation without enough information about soil conditions, which wastes water. Our solution uses available environmental data to recommend when and how much to irrigate." It says who, what and why.

B. Technical substance

TechniquePossible application
RegressionPredicting water or energy consumption
ClassificationIdentifying risk or abnormal conditions
ClusteringDiscovering consumption or usage patterns
Anomaly detectionDetecting unusual water or electricity usage
Time-series forecastingPredicting future demand
OptimizationEfficient resource allocation
Computer visionCrop conditions from images

Don't use all of them. If you can explain why your model fits, what it takes in, what it predicts and how the output changes a real decision, that beats "we used machine learning".

C. Measurable impact

Prepare at least one indicator: liters saved per irrigation cycle, % energy reduction in a defined scenario, anomalies correctly detected on a test set, forecast error versus a simple baseline, or time saved versus the current workflow. Separate measured from projected results.

Acceptable: "Under our simulated 100-hectare scenario, our engine estimates a potential 12% reduction in water use", with transparent assumptions.
Not acceptable: claiming 12% real-world savings when you only ran a simulation.

D. User experience

Judges should understand the app without explanations. Have one clear primary action, an understandable result, a meaningful visualization, a recommendation and a clear next step. Avoid extra menus, decorative graphs and walls of text.

E. Differentiation

Ask: why would someone use this instead of the existing method? Possible answers: more accessible, better local adaptation, lower infrastructure needs, predictive rather than reactive, combines normally separate data sources, a particularly useful workflow. A complicated architecture is not a unique value proposition.

6. AI and technical tricks

  1. Always establish a baseline. Regression: predict the mean or previous observation. Classification: majority class.
  2. Don't waste hours training unnecessarily. Look for existing datasets and pretrained models; start simple.
  3. Build the fallback before the fancy feature. One failed inference must never crash the demo.
  4. Use explainable outputs. Not just "Risk score: 0.89": say what it represents, which inputs contributed, the recommended action, and confidence if calibrated.
  5. Keep the pipeline reproducible. Save preprocessing, model config and sample inputs.
  6. Avoid AI for AI's sake. If rules fit better, use rules. Judges may ask why ML is needed.
  7. Don't overengineer. No Kubernetes, microservices or five agents for a 25-hour project.

7. The pitch

Pitch duration is not confirmed. Ask the organizers, prepare a three-minute version, then adapt.

TimePartContent
0:00-0:20HookMake the problem immediately understandable.
0:20-0:50ProblemWho, why it matters, evidence.
0:50-1:20SolutionYour product and its unique value.
1:20-2:20Live demoOne complete user journey and the actual result.
2:20-2:45Innovation and impactThe key technical decision and measurable benefit.
2:45-3:00ClosingOne memorable statement.

How to start

Never open with "Hello everyone, we are team X and today we present...". Open with a scenario, for example: "Imagine a farmer who must decide every morning whether to irrigate, without knowing how much water the soil needs. Every wrong decision costs water, money and productivity. We built a way to make that decision smarter."

The live demo

Show: the user opens the app, enters or selects information, the system processes it, the AI produces a result, the user gets a recommendation, and the measurable benefit appears. One complete scenario. Don't tour every page, don't show source code for 90 seconds, and don't explain architecture before anyone has seen the product.

How to close

Not "That's it, thank you." Connect technology to impact: "We didn't build another dashboard. We built a decision-support tool that turns available data into actionable resource-saving decisions." Only claim what the prototype supports.

Delivery tips

  • Speak slower than you think you need to; look at the judges, not the screen.
  • Don't read slides; keep them clean.
  • One main presenter; the technical owner is ready for detailed questions.
  • Never interrupt a teammate. If you don't know, say so and explain how you'd verify.

C. Project readiness: the definition of done

A working core journey, valid submission and reproducible demo matter more than perfect docs or sophisticated deployment.

Problem and product

  • The challenge statement is clearly understood.
  • The target user is identified.
  • The problem is specific and backed by evidence.
  • The solution addresses the assigned challenge.
  • The value proposition fits in one sentence.
  • The MVP scope is defined.
  • The main user journey is complete.

Technical implementation

  • Frontend, backend and AI/ML components run.
  • API integration works.
  • Database reads and writes work, if applicable.
  • Input validation exists and errors are handled gracefully.
  • The main feature works with realistic data.
  • Results are reproducible where expected.
  • No critical integration errors remain.

AI and data

  • Data sources are identified and licensing checked.
  • Preprocessing is reproducible.
  • The model or algorithm has a justified purpose and a baseline.
  • Evaluation is documented and accuracy claims are backed by tests.
  • Simulated and real data are clearly distinguished.
  • AI output leads to a useful action.

Impact and innovation

  • A clear differentiator exists.
  • Expected impact is explained with at least one measurable indicator.
  • Impact calculations have documented assumptions.
  • Limitations are acknowledged.
  • The solution is realistic beyond the hackathon.

Demo and deployment

  • The app launches from a clean environment.
  • The deployed app is accessible, if required.
  • The main demo scenario works start to finish, with demo data ready.
  • The UI is readable and consistent, with no broken buttons or dead links.
  • A backup demo recording and screenshots exist.
  • The demo was rehearsed on the actual presentation setup, if possible.

Submission and documentation

  • GitHub repository is accessible and all essential code is pushed.
  • README explains the project and how to run it.
  • Architecture and dependencies are documented.
  • External resources and AI tools are acknowledged as required.
  • Submission form and presentation materials are complete.
  • Submission receipt or confirmation is saved.

If the app breaks during the pitch

  1. Live application: show the real app and a complete journey.
  2. Local application: if deployment fails, run the working version locally.
  3. Recorded demo: if all else fails, play the recording and explain honestly.

Never pretend a recording is live. Ask the organizers whether recorded demos are allowed; some competitions require a live demo.

10. Questions for organizers and mentors

Organizers, at the forum

  1. What are the judging criteria and weights?
  2. Is there a maximum pitch duration?
  3. Is there a separate Q&A?
  4. What are the mandatory deliverables?
  5. Is a GitHub repository required?
  6. Are external APIs and pretrained models permitted?
  7. Are templates and boilerplate permitted?
  8. Any restrictions on generative AI?
  9. Is a live demo mandatory?
  10. What happens if internet or deployment fails?

Technical mentors

  • What are the most common technical mistakes in this domain?
  • Which datasets or sources do you recommend?
  • What would make this realistic in Tunisia?
  • What would prevent adoption?
  • Which metric best demonstrates effectiveness?

Domain experts

  • How is this problem solved today, and what is the biggest limitation?
  • Who makes the relevant decisions?
  • What would make this genuinely useful?
Best question: "If you were evaluating our solution, what would make you doubt that it could work in the real world?"

11. Your competitive advantage

Don't be the team with the most technologies. Be the team with the best mix of technical depth (justified AI/ML, good engineering, working implementation), problem relevance, measurable impact and communication.

  1. A real problem with a clear user.
  2. One complete working product journey.
  3. A technically defensible solution.
  4. A measurable impact demonstration.
  5. An excellent user interface.
  6. A polished presentation.
  7. Extra features only if everything above is secure.

Judges have limited time and may see many projects. Make it extremely easy to see why yours matters.
